# Top vs Bottom Story Patterns

This version makes the comparison process explicit.

1. Sort the full story set by `page_views`.
2. Compare the 25 highest- and 25 lowest-performing stories.
3. Tag each headline with the framing pattern that best explains why it worked or did not work.
4. Read the pattern column as the explanation layer, not just the topic label.

Comparison uses the top 25 and bottom 25 stories by `page_views`.

## What the Top Stories Have In Common

| Pattern | Evidence | Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete event or disruption | United U-turn, arrest, aircraft carrier problems, layoff, shutdown | The story is anchored in something that happened, not just a trend or topic. |
| Visible stakes | Money, travel, reputation, job security, military readiness | The headline makes the consequence legible immediately. |
| Recognizable anchor | Elon Musk, Don Lemon, Target, United, Navy, JPMorgan | A known person or institution gives the story instant relevance. |
| Conflict / surprise / reversal | Arrest fallout, weird travel incident, job regret, public firestorm | The hook is tension, not explanation. |
| Human-scale framing | Family moves, retirement abroad, unemployment, caregiving, price pressure | Large issues are made legible through personal consequences. |
| Scene-rich or vivid detail | Laptop down the seat side, combat/fire/plumbing problems, stationary planes overnight | Specific detail makes the story feel real and memorable. |
| Broadly legible at a glance | Reader can summarize in one sentence without industry jargon | The story can travel beyond the core vertical. |

## What the Bottom Stories Have In Common

| Pattern | Evidence | Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Guide / utility / shopping language | Best promo codes, best options, what to know, top picks | Useful, but often less urgent than a true event story. |
| Topic-first rather than event-first | AI trends, app-store rankings, market milestones, commentary | The headline signals a category more than a narrative. |
| Insider or industry framing | Wall Street gifting, tech debate, AI training data, product-builder language | The audience is narrower and the hook is more specialized. |
| Abstract consequence | Market levels, app rankings, model updates, product changes | The story explains what something means, but not always why to care now. |
| Less visible human stakes | Few personal, reputational, or immediate operational consequences in the headline | The emotional or practical payoff is weaker. |
| More explanation than event | Commentary and analysis dominate over scene or conflict | These stories often read as informative rather than urgent. |
| Trend/utility over tension | More guide, trend, or explainer structures | The hook is softer unless the angle is unusually sharp. |

## Practical Readout

- Top performers are usually built around a concrete incident, disruption, or personal/institutional consequence.
- Bottom performers are more likely to be utility-driven, trend-driven, or insider/analytical stories without a sharp event hook.
- The strongest repeated pattern is not topic alone; it is framing that makes the consequence immediate and easy to picture.
- If a story is about AI, markets, tech, or shopping, it usually needs a sharper event or human stake to break into the top tier.
